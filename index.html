Layer 2

Three foundations hold the cycle together

  • Enterprisewide governance

    Resilience is a shared responsibility, sponsored by the C-suite and overseen by the board. A steering committee and clear escalation lines keep oversight from slowing down a crisis response.

  • Cyber risk management

    Cyber risk sits inside enterprise risk management, with an agreed risk appetite, regular scenario exercises and baseline controls mapped to NIST CSF 2.0, ISO 27001, CIS 18 or NIST 800-53.

  • Minimum viable organisation

    Agree in advance the smallest set of functions the business needs to keep operating, then protect and recover those first. Everyone knows what comes first before the pressure starts.

Start with what the business cannot lose

Protecting everything equally spreads budgets thin and leaves critical services exposed. We begin with business impact, then test, assign and report against it.

  1. Rank what matters

    We use your business impact analysis, or help the business build one, to sort systems into mission-critical, business-critical and the rest. Business owners set recovery time and recovery point objectives they can live with.

    You get: a tiered list of critical services and a defined minimum viable organisation.

  2. Test against real attackers

    We start from the threat actors active in your sector, rank each technique as probable, plausible or possible, and test the likeliest paths in depth. Each path is scored on whether it was prevented, detected, contained and recovered from in production, not on paper.

    You get: evidence of which defences hold, and which fail, on the attacks most likely to reach you.

  3. Make ownership explicit

    Resilience spans security, IT, business continuity, risk, legal and communications. We help you agree who is responsible, accountable, supporting, consulted and informed for each activity, so no one finds the gaps mid-incident.

    You get: a resilience RASCI and a steering group with clear decision rights.

  4. Report in business terms

    Leadership sets the tolerance, we show what it costs to meet, and if that is too high you decide together what to accept. Each decision records the exposure before and after, the evidence, the cost and the owner.

    You get: board reporting tied to revenue, service and regulatory outcomes, not tool counts.

  5. Rehearse and adapt

    Tabletop exercises for executives, crisis teams and technical staff, plus full recovery drills. Lessons go straight back into response plans, business continuity plans and the impact analysis itself.

    You get: tracked time to detect, respond and recover, and a falling count of open gaps.

Questions we hear often

Something else on your mind? Ask a specialist.

What is cyber resilience?

It is your organisation's ability to keep delivering critical services during and after a cyber incident. It covers preventing attacks, but also detecting them quickly, limiting the damage and recovering in a planned way.

What do anticipate, withstand, recover and adapt mean?

They are the four stages of a continuous resilience cycle: anticipate threats, withstand attacks, recover quickly and adapt from what you learn. It rests on three foundations: enterprisewide governance, cyber risk management and a defined minimum viable organisation. See how we apply each layer.

How does it relate to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework?

They work together. NIST CSF 2.0 (govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, recover) defines the security controls you need. The resilience cycle asks whether those controls keep the business running during and after an attack. We map your controls to NIST CSF and measure outcomes against the resilience cycle.

What is a minimum viable organisation?

The smallest set of business functions you need to keep operating through a serious cyber crisis. Agreeing it in advance, usually from a business impact analysis, tells every team what to protect and restore first, so those decisions are not made under pressure.

Why start with a business impact analysis?

Without one, response plans follow technical priorities rather than business ones, and recovery targets are often unrealistic. A business impact analysis ranks critical processes, maps their dependencies and sets recovery time and recovery point objectives agreed with the business. Security investment can then follow that ranking.

Should our CISO own business continuity and disaster recovery?

Usually not on their own, even though more security leaders are being asked to. Security is best placed to own cyber controls, monitoring and cyber incident response. Business continuity often fits better with operations or enterprise risk, and IT disaster recovery with infrastructure teams. A shared RASCI makes the split explicit.

How is this different from a managed security service?

A managed security service usually focuses on monitoring and alerts. A resilience programme also covers governance, testing, response and recovery, with one team accountable for the outcome.

Do we have to replace our existing security tools?

No. We start from what you already run, integrate it where possible and only recommend changes where there is a clear gap.

Can we start with a single service?

Yes. Many clients begin with an assessment or a penetration test and extend into monitoring and response once they see the findings.

Which regulations do you help with?

Common ones include CERT-In Directions, SEBI's cyber resilience framework, RBI guidelines, the DPDP Act and ISO 27001. We map controls and evidence to whichever apply to you.

What happens if we are attacked right now?

Call the 24×7 incident hotline listed in the header and footer. A responder will help you contain the incident and preserve evidence, whether or not you are an existing client.

Is this suitable for small businesses?

Yes. Pre-configured packages give smaller teams the same lifecycle coverage without hiring in-house security staff.
